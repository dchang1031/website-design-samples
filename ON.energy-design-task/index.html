TECHNICAL BRIEF THERMAL ARCHITECTURE

Central Chiller Plant vs. the Rack-Level Thermal Buffer

Heat rejection, dynamic response, and data center uptime

BY JORDAN REYES

Both architectures keep racks cool at a steady load. They split apart the moment something changes. A training cluster spikes. A chiller trips. This brief walks through where they split, and why it matters for an AI factory.

01 THE PROBLEM

Two answers to the same heat spike

AI training clusters produce heat in sharp, fast bursts. A single rack can swing from 40 kW to 130 kW in seconds. Cooling designs built for steady enterprise loads are now being tested against that pattern. Two approaches lead the field. The central chiller plant cools the whole hall from one shared loop. The rack-level thermal buffer stores cooling capacity at each row, ready before the heat arrives.

Both hold temperature at a steady load. They differ sharply in response speed, fault tolerance, and commissioning effort. This brief makes five points:

01

Central plants respond to heat spikes after a lag, estimated at 20 to 45 seconds, as chilled water travels the loop.

02

That lag is manageable at moderate density. At 100 kW per rack and above, it pushes inlet temperatures past safe limits.

03

A chiller trip forces a hard choice. Protect the hottest racks, or protect the plant’s restart sequence.

04

The common workaround stacks storage tanks, backup pumps, and a second chiller. Each layer adds cost, footprint, and failure points.

05

A rack-level thermal buffer absorbs spikes where they start, keeps racks cool through a plant trip, and reduces commissioning to one repeatable model.

02 ARCHITECTURE

Where the cold is stored changes everything

The two systems can use the same chillers and the same coolant. The difference is location. Cold capacity either lives at a central plant and travels to the racks, or it sits at the row and waits for the heat.

Central Chiller Plant

CENTRALIZED · SHARED LOOP

One shared loop. A spike in one row pulls on the whole plant. A plant fault reaches every row at once.

  • Chillers sit in a central plant and feed every row through one loop.
  • Response depends on pump speed, pipe length, and valve timing. Faster controls shorten the lag. They cannot remove it.
  • Every rack shares the same supply temperature.
  • Defining trait: every row depends on the same loop, in real time.

Rack-Level Thermal Buffer

DISTRIBUTED · ROW-LEVEL

Cold is stored at the row. The plant recharges the buffer. The racks draw from the buffer.

  • A phase-change thermal store sits at the end of each row.
  • The central plant recharges the buffers at a steady, planned rate.
  • Local controls match cooling output to rack heat in real time.
  • Defining trait: stable inlet temperature, independent of plant events.

04 PLANT DISTURBANCE

One event, five endings: the chiller trip

A power fault takes the lead chiller offline. The backup needs 8 to 12 minutes to reach full capacity. The racks need cold water every second. A central plant has to decide which need comes first. Each fix for that decision creates the next problem.

STANDBY ROWS OVERHEAT

Protect the hottest racks

CONFIGURATION 1 Remaining capacity diverted to high-density rows

The AI rows stayed cool. The rest of the hall did not. Protecting the compute created a new hot spot.

t = 0 s

Lead chiller trips offline.

t = 2–5 s

The building management system detects the loss of flow and flags the event.

t = 5–20 s

Valves close on standard rows and send all remaining chilled water to the AI rows.

t = 60 s +

Standard rows run warm. Storage and network gear approach alarm thresholds.

COMPUTE THROTTLED

Protect the restart

CONFIGURATION 2 Flow held balanced across every row

The plant restarted cleanly. Training jobs lost ten minutes and fell back to their last checkpoint. The step-by-step ramp is complex to model and harder to run at scale.

t = 0 s

Lead chiller trips offline.

t = 2–5 s

The building management system detects the loss of flow.

t = 10–30 s

GPU clusters throttle to 40% to cut heat output.

t = 8–12 min

The backup chiller reaches capacity. Workloads ramp back up in steps matched to the returning cooling, until the hall is back to full load.

NO THERMAL EVENT

Nothing to divert, nothing to rush

SAME EVENT · THERMAL BUFFER Row-level buffers on every row

Zero throttling. No valve choreography. Compute and support equipment stay within limits the whole time.

t = 0 s

Lead chiller trips offline.

t = 0 s

Racks keep drawing from the row buffer. They never drew directly from the plant, so there is nothing to detect and nothing to redirect.

t = 0 → 20 min

The buffer holds inlet temperature steady and recharges as soon as plant capacity returns.

08 COMMISSIONING

The model the operator sees

Commissioning is where design complexity turns into schedule. A central plant with add-ons hands the operator a web of interacting behaviors: valve sequences, tank dispatch, backup pump transfer, and the stepped restart ramp. Each behavior needs its own control model. Each fault scenario needs its own test, sign-off, and retest after every change.

The buffered hall reduces to one repeatable row model. Test it once, and it holds for every row in the hall.

Model the loop and everything on it

CENTRAL PLANT · COMMISSIONING SCOPE

Every row shares the loop, so the test plan must cover all of it: the chillers and their restart logic, plus the tank, pumps, valves, and air handlers. Each has its own control model, and each is tested against every fault scenario.

Model one row

THERMAL BUFFER · COMMISSIONING SCOPE

The test covers one row model, repeated across the hall. Everything behind the buffer is shielded from plant events, so it never enters the test plan.

From the operator’s side, the hall never throttles, never reroutes, and never runs a restart ramp. It is one steady, predictable load on the plant.

To the operator, a hall behind thermal buffers is the simplest system there is. A plant that only recharges.
